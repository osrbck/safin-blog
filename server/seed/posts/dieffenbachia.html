Dieffenbachia, the "dumb cane", is an easy houseplant whose sap holds needle-like crystals that cause painful mouth swelling in children and pets.

Not every poisonous plant lives in a dark wood or a forgotten corner of the garden. Some sit in a pot by the sofa. Dieffenbachia, with its broad, glossy leaves splashed in cream and lime green, has been a staple of living rooms, offices, hotel lobbies and dentists' waiting rooms for generations. It tolerates low light, forgives the occasional missed watering and grows into a handsome, tropical-looking specimen. It also has one of the most vivid nicknames in the plant world, and the story behind that name is worth knowing if you share your home with small children or pets.

Why "dumb cane"?

The genus was named by the Austrian botanist Heinrich Wilhelm Schott, director of the imperial gardens in Vienna, in honour of Joseph Dieffenbach, the head gardener at Schönbrunn Palace. The English nickname is far less flattering. Bite into a stem and your mouth and tongue can swell and burn so painfully that speaking becomes difficult, leaving you temporarily "dumb", an old word for unable to speak. Historical accounts from Caribbean plantations, discussed in ethnobotanical literature, claim that the plant was even used as a cruel punishment. Today the nickname survives mainly as a warning.

How to identify dieffenbachia

Dieffenbachia is a member of the arum family (Araceae), the same family as philodendrons, pothos, peace lilies and monsteras. Common houseplant varieties share these features:

  • Stems: thick, upright, fleshy and cane-like, with visible rings where old leaves have fallen. Older plants can become leggy and reach a metre or more indoors.
  • Leaves: large, oval to lance-shaped, glossy and often heavily variegated with white, cream or yellow blotches, streaks or speckles. Popular cultivars include 'Camille', 'Tropic Snow' and 'Compacta'.
  • Flowers: rare indoors; a pale green, hooded spathe surrounding a finger-like spadix, typical of the arum family.
  • Sap: clear and watery when a leaf or stem is cut.

It is often confused with Chinese evergreen (Aglaonema), another variegated aroid. Chinese evergreen tends to be smaller, with narrower leaves, and it can also cause oral irritation, so the same precautions apply to both.

Where it grows

In the wild, dieffenbachias are understorey plants of tropical forests in Central and South America and the Caribbean. They have been grown in Europe since the nineteenth century and are now sold almost everywhere as houseplants. In frost-free climates they are also grown outdoors in shady gardens and can naturalise in moist woodland.

What makes it irritating

Dieffenbachia's defence is mechanical as much as chemical. Its tissues contain specialised cells called idioblasts, packed with bundles of microscopic, needle-shaped crystals of calcium oxalate, known as raphides. When the leaf or stem is chewed or crushed, pressure fires these needles out into the surrounding tissue. They pierce the delicate lining of the lips, tongue and throat, and irritant compounds in the sap appear to make the effect worse, triggering rapid pain and swelling.

The good news is that most exposures are mild. A poison information specialist quoted by fact-checkers noted that the majority of cases develop only mild symptoms, and data from the US National Poison Data System cited in the same report found no deaths from oxalate plant exposures in 2022. The pain itself usually stops people, and animals, from eating very much.

Symptoms in people

  • Immediate burning or stinging pain in the mouth and on the lips.
  • Swelling of the tongue, lips and throat; drooling; difficulty speaking.
  • Blistering inside the mouth.
  • Pain or difficulty swallowing; occasionally nausea or vomiting.
  • If sap gets in the eyes: intense pain, watering, redness and light sensitivity, with a risk of injury to the cornea.
  • Skin irritation or a rash in people with sensitive skin who handle cut stems.

Rarely, swelling can be severe enough to threaten the airway. Published case reports include an adult who bit into a stem and needed an emergency airway procedure before making a full recovery. Any difficulty breathing is an emergency.

Symptoms in pets

The ASPCA lists dieffenbachia as toxic to dogs and cats. Pets that chew it typically show:

  • Pawing at the mouth or face, head shaking.
  • Heavy drooling and foaming.
  • Vomiting.
  • Reluctance to eat, or difficulty swallowing.
  • Swelling of the lips, tongue or muzzle.

Cats that like to nibble leaves and puppies that chew everything are the usual culprits. Rabbits, guinea pigs and pet birds that roam the house can also be affected.

Who is most at risk?

  • Babies and toddlers, who put leaves in their mouths and cannot explain what has happened.
  • Cats, dogs and other indoor pets, especially young or bored ones.
  • Plant owners who prune or propagate without washing their hands and then rub their eyes.

Living safely with dieffenbachia

  1. Place it high or out of the way, on a stand, shelf or in a room pets and toddlers cannot reach unsupervised.
  2. Wear gloves when pruning, repotting or taking cuttings, and consider eye protection if you are cutting thick canes.
  3. Wash your hands and tools afterwards, and do not touch your eyes or mouth while working.
  4. Clean up fallen leaves promptly; a dropped leaf on the floor is the classic route to a curious pet.
  5. Choose pet-friendly alternatives if you have a determined plant-chewer. Calatheas, spider plants and Boston ferns are commonly listed as non-toxic to cats and dogs by the ASPCA.
  6. Remember its relatives. Philodendron, pothos, peace lily, monstera and calla lily contain similar oxalate crystals and deserve the same respect.

What to do in an emergency

If someone has bitten or chewed dieffenbachia:

  1. Remove any plant pieces from the mouth and wipe the mouth gently with a cool, wet cloth.
  2. Rinse the mouth with water. If the person can swallow normally, poison centres such as those advising through MedlinePlus may suggest sips of cold water or milk. Do not give anything by mouth if they are vomiting, drowsy or struggling to swallow.
  3. For eye exposure, rinse the eye gently with plenty of clean, lukewarm running water and get medical advice.
  4. Do not induce vomiting unless a medical professional tells you to.

Then call for advice:

  • Türkiye: 112, or the Ulusal Zehir Danışma Merkezi on 114, around the clock.
  • United States: Poison Help on 1-800-222-1222; call 911 immediately for any swelling of the throat or trouble breathing.
  • United Kingdom: 999 in an emergency or NHS 111. Elsewhere in Europe, 112.
  • Pets: your vet or an emergency clinic; in the US, the ASPCA Animal Poison Control Center on (888) 426-4435 (a fee may apply). Swelling of the muzzle or any breathing difficulty needs urgent veterinary attention.

Have the plant or a photo of it to hand, and note the person's or animal's age, weight and when the exposure happened.

Key facts

  • Botanical name: Dieffenbachia species, often D. seguine (family Araceae).
  • Irritant: needle-like calcium oxalate crystals (raphides) fired from idioblast cells, plus irritant sap.
  • Main effect: immediate mouth pain and swelling; eye injury if sap gets in the eyes.
  • Usual outcome: mild, but airway swelling is a rare emergency.
  • Pets: toxic to dogs and cats.
  • Relatives with similar crystals: philodendron, pothos, peace lily, monstera, Chinese evergreen.
