Lily of the valley stars in May Day posies and royal bouquets, but it holds heart toxins and is often mistaken for wild garlic. Learn the difference.

There are flowers you notice with your eyes and flowers you notice with your nose first. Lily of the valley belongs firmly to the second group. Walk past a shady patch in late spring and a sweet, clean perfume stops you in your tracks; look down and there they are, arching stems of tiny white bells tucked between broad green leaves. It is one of the most beloved flowers in Europe. It is also one of the most important plants for foragers and parents to recognise, because beneath its innocent look lies a set of potent heart-affecting compounds.

A flower of luck, love and May Day

Convallaria majalis carries a name that roughly means "of the valley, flowering in May", and folklore has wrapped it in good fortune. In France, sprigs of muguet are sold on street corners and given to friends and family on 1 May as a token of luck for the year ahead. Christian legends called it "Our Lady's tears", imagining the white bells springing from Mary's weeping at the cross. Christian Dior adored the flower and wove it into his collections and perfumes, and it has long been a favourite in bridal bouquets, including the one carried by Catherine Middleton at her 2011 wedding to Prince William. Perfumers have worked for generations to capture its scent.

Despite its common name, lily of the valley is not a true lily. It belongs to the asparagus family, Asparagaceae. That distinction matters for cat owners, as we will see below.

How to identify lily of the valley

  • Leaves: usually two, sometimes three, broad, smooth, elliptical leaves with parallel veins, emerging from the ground tightly rolled together. At the base they are wrapped in a sheath that is often reddish or purplish.
  • Flowers: a single leafless stem carrying five to fifteen small, white, nodding, bell-shaped flowers, all on one side. They are intensely fragrant.
  • Berries: after flowering, round berries that turn from green to a bright orange-red in late summer.
  • Roots: creeping underground rhizomes that let the plant form dense carpets.

The wild garlic problem

Every spring, foragers across Europe head into damp woodland to gather wild garlic, also called ramsons or bear's garlic (Allium ursinum), a delicious green that is increasingly popular in pesto and soups. In Türkiye it is gathered in the Black Sea region as ayı sarımsağı. The trouble is that young lily of the valley leaves emerge at the same time, in similar shady places, and look remarkably alike. Germany's Federal Institute for Risk Assessment (BfR) warns that confusion between wild garlic and poisonous look-alikes, including lily of the valley and autumn crocus, leads to poisonings every season, some of them fatal.

How to tell them apart:

  • Smell: crushed wild garlic leaves smell strongly of garlic. Lily of the valley leaves have no garlic smell. But beware: once your fingers smell of garlic, every leaf you crush afterwards will seem to as well. The BfR notes that the smell test has limits.
  • Stems: each wild garlic leaf grows on its own individual stalk directly from the ground. Lily of the valley leaves come in pairs wrapped around a single stem, often with a reddish sheath at the base, as Norway's Poison Information Centre highlights.
  • Flowers: wild garlic produces loose, starry clusters of white flowers; lily of the valley produces nodding white bells.
  • Veins and stalk colour: the parallel veins on a wild garlic leaf sit further apart than those on lily of the valley, and wild garlic stalks often fade from pale green to white towards the ground.

Pick leaf by leaf, never by the handful, and if there is any doubt, leave it. As the BfR puts it, if in doubt, it is better not to eat wild garlic you have gathered yourself.

Where it grows

Lily of the valley is native to cool temperate parts of Europe and Asia, growing in deciduous woodland, especially on well-drained soils in light shade. It is a hugely popular garden plant and has escaped cultivation in many places, including North America, where it can form large colonies in shady yards and old gardens.

What makes it dangerous

All parts of the plant, including flowers, leaves, berries and rhizomes, contain cardiac glycosides, the best known of which is convallatoxin, along with saponins. Like the compounds in foxglove and oleander, cardiac glycosides affect the electrical activity of the heart. The saponins add stomach irritation.

It is worth keeping the risk in perspective. Austria's food safety agency AGES notes that life-threatening poisoning is rare, and Norwegian poison specialists point out that simply tasting a tiny piece is unlikely to cause severe poisoning. The real danger lies in eating a larger amount, for example a whole meal of leaves mistaken for wild garlic.

Symptoms in people

  • Nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain and diarrhoea.
  • Dizziness, weakness, confusion or drowsiness.
  • Blurred or altered vision.
  • Palpitations, a slow or irregular heartbeat.

Symptoms in pets

The ASPCA lists lily of the valley as toxic to dogs, cats and horses. Signs include vomiting, a slow or irregular heart rate, a drop in blood pressure, disorientation and, in severe cases, seizures or collapse. Cats deserve special mention: although lily of the valley is not one of the true lilies (Lilium and Hemerocallis) that cause kidney failure in cats, it is dangerous to them for a different reason, its effect on the heart. Bouquets containing it should be kept well away from curious cats.

Who is most at risk?

  • Wild garlic foragers in spring, especially beginners.
  • Toddlers, who may be drawn to the bright orange-red berries in late summer.
  • Cats and dogs in homes with cut flowers or gardens with dense patches.
  • Gardeners who grow lily of the valley next to herbs or edible greens.

Safe gardening and handling tips

  1. Keep it out of the kitchen garden. Never plant lily of the valley in the same bed as wild garlic, chives or other edible leaves.
  2. Wash your hands after handling the plant or arranging bouquets.
  3. Put bouquets out of reach of children and pets, and discard the vase water safely rather than leaving it where an animal might drink it.
  4. Pick off berries in late summer if young children use the garden.
  5. Contain its spread. Its rhizomes travel; a root barrier or a dedicated shady corner keeps it where you want it.
  6. Forage responsibly. Learn wild garlic with an experienced guide, check every leaf, and do not rely on smell alone.

What to do in an emergency

If someone may have eaten lily of the valley, whether berries, flowers or leaves in a mistaken wild garlic dish, seek advice promptly.

  • Türkiye: call 112, or the Ulusal Zehir Danışma Merkezi (National Poison Information Centre) on 114, open 24 hours.
  • United States: Poison Help, 1-800-222-1222; call 911 for fainting, collapse or breathing problems.
  • United Kingdom: 999 in an emergency or NHS 111. Elsewhere in Europe, dial 112.
  • Pets: call your vet or nearest emergency clinic. In the US, the ASPCA Animal Poison Control Center is on (888) 426-4435 (a fee may apply).

Bring a sample of the plant, any leftover food, or a photo. Do not induce vomiting unless a poison centre or doctor advises it. If several people shared the same meal, all of them should be assessed.

Key facts

  • Botanical name: Convallaria majalis (family Asparagaceae); not a true lily.
  • Toxic parts: all parts, including flowers, leaves, berries and rhizomes.
  • Main toxins: cardiac glycosides such as convallatoxin, plus saponins.
  • Classic look-alike: wild garlic (ramsons) in spring.
  • Quick ID clue: two leaves wrapped around one stem with a reddish sheath; no garlic smell.
  • Pets: toxic to dogs, cats and horses.
