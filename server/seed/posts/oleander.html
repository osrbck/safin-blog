Oleander lines Mediterranean roads with bright blooms, yet every part of this tough evergreen carries heart toxins. Here is how to enjoy it safely.

Drive along almost any coastal highway in Türkiye in June and you will see it: a ribbon of oleander running down the central reservation, flowering cheerfully in heat and dust that would finish off most garden plants. In Turkish it is zakkum; in Spain, adelfa; to botanists, Nerium oleander. It is drought-proof, salt-tolerant, almost pest-free and beautiful for months on end. It is also one of the most toxic ornamental plants in common cultivation, and that combination of ubiquity and danger is exactly why it deserves a closer look.

A plant the ancients already respected

Oleander has been planted around the Mediterranean for thousands of years, and classical writers knew it was not to be trifled with. Pliny the Elder, writing in the first century, noted that the shrub the Greeks called rhododendron was a poison to grazing animals such as goats and sheep. Folklore has added plenty of embroidery since. One much-repeated tale claims that soldiers in the Napoleonic Wars fell ill after roasting meat on oleander skewers. The story is almost certainly apocryphal, but its persistence tells you something: people around the Mediterranean have long passed down the warning that this pretty hedge is not harmless.

How to identify oleander

Oleander is an evergreen shrub or small tree, usually two to four metres tall in gardens, though it can grow larger when left alone. Look for:

  • Leaves that are long, narrow and leathery, dark green above and paler beneath, with a prominent central vein. They usually grow in whorls of three around the stem.
  • Flowers in clusters at the branch tips, each with five petals that look slightly twisted, like a pinwheel. Colours range from white and pale pink to deep rose, red, apricot and yellow. Double-flowered varieties are common.
  • Seed pods that are long and slender, splitting open to release seeds with tufts of silky hair.
  • Sap that is clear to slightly milky and sticky when a stem is snapped.

Its closest look-alike in warm countries is yellow oleander (Cascabela thevetia), a related shrub with yellow, trumpet-shaped flowers. It is also highly toxic. Out of flower, oleander's narrow, leathery leaves can look like those of several harmless evergreens to an untrained eye, which is one more reason never to pick leaves for cooking, wrapping food or brewing tea from a shrub you cannot name with certainty.

Where it grows

Native to a broad band from the Mediterranean basin to parts of Asia, oleander grows wild along seasonal riverbeds and gravelly stream banks. It has been planted throughout the warm regions of the world, from southern Europe and the Middle East to California, Australia and South Asia. In Türkiye you will find it on road medians, seaside promenades, hotel gardens and school grounds from İzmir to Antalya and beyond. In cooler climates it is grown in pots and moved indoors for winter.

What makes it dangerous

All parts of the plant contain cardiac glycosides, the best known of which is oleandrin. These compounds interfere with the sodium-potassium pump that heart muscle cells rely on to keep a steady rhythm. The result can be a slowed or chaotic heartbeat and dangerous changes in blood potassium. The same family of chemicals is found in foxglove, and doctors manage oleander poisoning in a similar way to an overdose of the heart drug digoxin.

Several features make oleander especially hazardous:

  • Drying does not make it safe. Extension specialists at New Mexico State University note that the toxins remain in dried leaves, which can collect in pasture grass or end up in hay.
  • Burning is a bad idea. The same experts advise against burning oleander because the toxic compounds can be carried in the smoke.
  • Wilted clippings are palatable to livestock, so a pile of hedge trimmings can be more tempting to a horse or goat than the living shrub.

Symptoms in people

Poisoning usually follows chewing or swallowing leaves, flowers or seeds. Early signs are digestive: nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain and diarrhoea. These can be followed by dizziness, weakness, confusion, visual disturbances and, most seriously, a slow, irregular or racing heartbeat. Touching the plant is generally harmless for most people, though the sap can irritate sensitive skin and eyes.

Symptoms in pets and livestock

The ASPCA lists oleander as toxic to dogs, cats and horses. Signs include drooling, vomiting, diarrhoea, abdominal pain or colic, depression and abnormal heart rhythms. Horses, cattle, goats and sheep are frequently poisoned when clippings are thrown over a fence or leaves fall into feed. Kidney damage has also been reported in horses.

Who is most at risk?

  • Toddlers, who explore by mouth and are drawn to bright flowers.
  • Dogs and cats, especially puppies and bored pets chewing on garden plants or fallen leaves.
  • Horses and farm animals near hedges, or exposed to trimmings and contaminated hay.
  • Gardeners who prune, burn or compost large amounts of the plant without precautions.
  • People experimenting with herbal remedies. Oleander is sometimes promoted online as a natural cure. It is not safe to take in any home-made form.

Growing oleander safely

None of this means you must rip out every oleander on your street. It means treating the plant with the respect you would give a sharp tool.

  1. Wear gloves and long sleeves when pruning, and keep the sap away from your eyes and mouth. Wash your hands afterwards.
  2. Bag clippings and dispose of them with household or municipal green waste. Do not burn them, and do not leave piles where animals can reach them.
  3. Never use the wood for skewers, barbecue fuel or children's crafts.
  4. Think twice about planting it beside play areas, paddocks, chicken runs or where a puppy will spend long unsupervised hours.
  5. Teach children early that garden flowers and leaves are for looking at, not tasting.
  6. If you keep horses, walk your fence lines and check that no neighbouring oleander overhangs a paddock or water trough.

What to do in an emergency

If you think a person or animal has eaten any part of an oleander, act immediately. Do not wait for symptoms, because heart effects can develop over several hours.

  • Türkiye: call 112, or the National Poison Information Centre (Ulusal Zehir Danışma Merkezi) on 114, which answers 24 hours a day.
  • United States: call Poison Help on 1-800-222-1222, or 911 if the person has collapsed, is having a seizure or is struggling to breathe.
  • United Kingdom: call 999 in an emergency or NHS 111 for advice. Elsewhere in Europe, 112 reaches emergency services.
  • Pets and livestock: call your vet or an emergency clinic straight away. In the US, the ASPCA Animal Poison Control Center is on (888) 426-4435 (a consultation fee may apply).

Take a sample of the plant or a clear photo with you, note roughly when the exposure happened, and remove any plant material left in the mouth. Do not make the person or animal vomit unless a poison centre or doctor specifically tells you to. Hospitals have effective treatments, including specific antibody therapy that can counter digoxin-like poisons, and the sooner they start, the better.

Key facts

  • Botanical name: Nerium oleander (family Apocynaceae); Turkish zakkum.
  • Toxic parts: all of them, fresh or dried, including the sap.
  • Main toxins: cardiac glycosides such as oleandrin.
  • Main danger: disturbed heart rhythm after swallowing plant material.
  • Affected animals: dogs, cats, horses, cattle, goats, sheep and others.
  • Never burn it, cook with its wood, or brew it into tea.
