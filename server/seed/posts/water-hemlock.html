Water hemlock resembles wild carrot and parsnip and grows by ditches and rivers, yet it is among North America's most toxic plants. Foragers, take note.

Spend a summer afternoon by a slow river or a marshy ditch and you will probably meet a member of the carrot family. Lacy white umbrellas of tiny flowers sway above feathery or toothed leaves: wild carrot, cow parsley, angelica, water parsnip. Many of these plants are harmless, a few are edible, and one or two are among the most dangerous things growing in the temperate world. Water hemlock, the Cicuta genus, belongs in the last group. The US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention has described spotted water hemlock as "the most toxic indigenous plant in North America". If you forage, garden near water or keep livestock, it is a plant worth knowing intimately.

Not Socrates' hemlock

The word "hemlock" causes plenty of confusion. The poison that killed the philosopher Socrates in 399 BCE is generally identified as poison hemlock (Conium maculatum), a different plant in the same family with its own dangerous toxins. Water hemlock is a separate genus. In North America the best-known species is spotted water hemlock (Cicuta maculata), with western water hemlock (Cicuta douglasii) further west; in Europe and northern Asia the main species is cowbane (Cicuta virosa). That old English name, cowbane, tells you who has historically paid the price: grazing animals.

How to identify water hemlock

Water hemlock is a tall perennial, often one to two metres high. No single feature should be relied on, but together these are telling:

  • Flowers: flat-topped to slightly domed umbrellas (umbels) of small white flowers in summer, made up of many smaller umbrellas.
  • Stems: smooth and hairless, hollow between the joints, and often streaked or blotched with purple, especially near the base.
  • Leaves: divided two or three times into narrow, lance-shaped, sharply toothed leaflets.
  • The vein test: on many leaflets, the side veins run to the notches between the teeth rather than to the tips of the teeth. Several botanical guides describe this as one of the most useful field marks, though it is not foolproof on every leaf.
  • The base and roots: the swollen base of the stem, when cut lengthwise, shows horizontal chambers separated by thin walls. The roots are a cluster of thick, fleshy, finger-like tubers. Cut surfaces can ooze a yellowish, oily liquid with a strong smell. These features are diagnostic, but examining them means handling the most toxic part of the plant, so leave it to experts with gloves.

Look-alikes

The CDC notes that water hemlock is similar in appearance to parsnips and belongs to the same family as parsley, celery and carrots. In the field, it can be confused with:

  • Wild carrot or Queen Anne's lace (Daucus carota), which has hairy stems, finely divided feathery leaves and often a single dark floret in the middle of the umbel.
  • Water parsnip (Sium suave) and cow parsnip (Heracleum species), which share wet habitats.
  • Angelica, prized by some foragers.
  • Wild ginseng and other edible roots. In 1992, two brothers foraging for wild ginseng in Maine dug up a root and ate it. It was water hemlock. One brother died within hours; the other survived after seizures and hospital treatment.
  • Poison hemlock, which is equally off-limits.

The lesson professional foragers repeat is blunt: unless you have been trained by an expert and are absolutely certain, do not eat any wild plant from the carrot family. The edible members are simply not worth the risk of a mistake.

Where it grows

True to its name, water hemlock likes its feet wet. Look for it in marshes, wet meadows, ditches, pond and lake margins, riverbanks and the edges of irrigation channels. It occurs widely across North America and, in the case of cowbane, across much of northern and central Europe and Asia. Disturbed, muddy ground where roots are easily pulled up, such as trampled stream banks after spring floods, is where livestock most often encounter it.

What makes it dangerous

Water hemlock contains cicutoxin and related compounds, which act on the central nervous system and cause violent seizures. The toxin is found throughout the plant, but the CDC reports that the roots contain the highest concentration. Even a small amount of root can cause severe or fatal poisoning in an adult, and symptoms can begin within minutes. The CDC notes that no antidote exists; treatment is supportive, focused on controlling seizures and protecting breathing.

Symptoms in people

  • Rapid onset, sometimes within 15 to 60 minutes.
  • Heavy salivation, nausea, vomiting and abdominal pain.
  • Tremors, muscle twitching, dilated pupils and confusion.
  • Violent, repeated seizures, which may become continuous.
  • Breathing difficulty, an abnormal heart rhythm and loss of consciousness.

Symptoms in animals

Cattle, sheep, goats and horses are poisoned most often, usually in spring when fresh growth and roots are exposed in soft, wet soil. Affected animals may drool, twitch, convulse and die quickly; sometimes they are simply found dead near a ditch or pond. Dogs can be affected if they chew roots, for example while digging along a riverbank.

Who is most at risk?

  • Foragers looking for wild carrot, parsnip, ginseng, angelica or other edible roots.
  • Children playing near water; never let them use hollow stems as whistles, straws or pea-shooters.
  • Farmers and horse owners with wet pastures, drainage ditches or stream access.
  • Landowners and volunteers clearing wetland weeds by hand.

Safe handling and land-management tips

  1. Never taste or nibble any part of an unknown plant from the carrot family. There is no safe "taste test".
  2. Wear waterproof gloves, long sleeves and eye protection if you must remove plants, and wash thoroughly afterwards.
  3. Remove the whole plant, roots included, bag it and dispose of it according to local rules. Do not leave uprooted plants lying where livestock or dogs can reach them.
  4. Fence off wet areas where water hemlock grows, especially in early spring.
  5. Do not feed animals on hungry, overgrazed pasture next to ditches; hungry animals are less selective.
  6. Ask for help. Local extension services and agricultural advisers can confirm identification and recommend control methods.

What to do in an emergency

Suspected water hemlock poisoning is a true emergency. Call for help immediately; do not wait to see whether symptoms develop.

  • Türkiye: 112, or the Ulusal Zehir Danışma Merkezi on 114, 24 hours a day.
  • United States: 911 first if there are any symptoms, then Poison Help on 1-800-222-1222.
  • United Kingdom: 999; elsewhere in Europe, 112.
  • Animals: call your vet at once; in the US, the ASPCA Animal Poison Control Center is on (888) 426-4435 (a fee may apply).

If someone is having a seizure, keep them away from hard objects and water, place them on their side once the jerking stops, and do not put anything in their mouth. Bring a sample of the plant in a sealed bag, or a photo, but do not delay to collect it. Do not induce vomiting.

Key facts

  • Botanical names: Cicuta maculata, C. douglasii, C. virosa (family Apiaceae).
  • Main toxin: cicutoxin, concentrated in the roots.
  • Main danger: rapid, violent seizures; no antidote.
  • Habitat: wet ground: ditches, marshes, stream and pond edges.
  • ID clues: purple-streaked hollow stems, toothed leaflets with veins ending in the notches, chambered stem base.
  • Rule for foragers: never eat a wild carrot-family plant without expert confirmation.
