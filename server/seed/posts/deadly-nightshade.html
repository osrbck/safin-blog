Deadly nightshade's glossy black berries look sweet enough to eat. Learn to recognise belladonna, why children are most at risk, and what to do.

Few plant names carry as much menace as "deadly nightshade", and few plants have earned it so thoroughly. Atropa belladonna has been entangled with poisoners, witches and court beauties in European folklore for centuries. Yet in the real world its victims are rarely the subject of drama. They are much more often small children who find a shrub hung with shiny black berries at the edge of a wood and do what small children do. Knowing this plant by sight is one of the simplest ways to keep a family walk from ending in an emergency room.

A name steeped in myth

When Linnaeus named the genus in the eighteenth century, he chose Atropa, after Atropos, the one of the three Fates in Greek myth who cuts the thread of life. The species name, belladonna, is Italian for "beautiful lady". According to a tradition repeated in countless herbals, women in Renaissance Italy used drops made from the plant to widen their pupils, which was considered alluring. Whatever the truth of that story, the effect on the eye is real: one of the plant's alkaloids, atropine, is still used by eye doctors today, in carefully controlled pharmaceutical form, to dilate pupils for examination. In Turkish the plant is known as güzelavratotu, which also translates roughly as "beautiful woman's herb".

How to identify deadly nightshade

Belladonna is a bushy, branching perennial that can reach 1.5 metres or more. Key features:

  • Leaves: large, oval, pointed and soft, with smooth edges. They often grow in unequal pairs, one large leaf beside a smaller one.
  • Flowers: solitary, nodding bells about two to three centimetres long, a dull purple-brown or violet, sometimes tinged green. They appear from early summer.
  • Berries: cherry-sized, glossy and green at first, ripening to a shining purple-black. Each berry sits alone on a stalk, cupped by a distinctive green, five-pointed star-shaped calyx. That green star is one of the plant's most reliable signatures.

Look-alikes worth knowing:

  • Black nightshade (Solanum nigrum), a common garden weed, has much smaller black berries carried in clusters, and small white star-shaped flowers with yellow centres. It is also considered toxic, especially when unripe.
  • Bittersweet (Solanum dulcamara), a scrambling vine sometimes called "woody nightshade", has purple flowers with yellow beaks and clusters of oval red berries.
  • Edible berries such as bilberries, blueberries and black cherries. Belladonna berries are larger than bilberries, grow singly rather than in bunches, and sit on that unmistakable green star.

Where it grows

Deadly nightshade is native to Europe, North Africa and western Asia, including Türkiye, where it grows in forests and clearings, notably in the Black Sea region. It favours shady, disturbed ground on chalk or limestone: woodland edges, quarries, old hedgerows, and the rubble around ruins and abandoned buildings, a habit that has only added to its gothic reputation. It has also become established in parts of North America and elsewhere.

A hazard in the spinach bunch

In Türkiye, belladonna has turned up in a surprisingly ordinary place: the vegetable market. In the autumn of 2021, Turkish media reported a wave of illnesses initially blamed on spinach. Emergency physicians at Karadeniz Technical University in Trabzon explained that the spinach itself was not the problem; young güzelavratotu leaves had been harvested and bundled together with it. Patients developed dilated pupils, a dry mouth, flushed skin, a racing pulse and hallucinations within an hour or two of eating. The doctors' advice was refreshingly practical: sort and wash market greens leaf by leaf, and throw out anything that does not look like the rest of the bunch.

What makes it dangerous

The roots, leaves and berries all contain tropane alkaloids, mainly atropine, hyoscyamine and scopolamine. These block acetylcholine, a chemical messenger that the nervous system uses to control everything from heart rate and digestion to sweating, salivation and focusing the eyes. When those signals are blocked, the body's "rest and digest" functions shut down and the brain becomes overstimulated. Medical case reports note that children are particularly sensitive: a small amount can produce serious effects.

Symptoms in people

Doctors learn a memorable rhyme to describe this kind of poisoning: "hot as a hare, blind as a bat, dry as a bone, red as a beet, mad as a hatter." In practice that means:

  • Flushed, hot, dry skin and a raised temperature.
  • Widely dilated pupils and blurred vision; bright light may hurt.
  • A very dry mouth and difficulty swallowing.
  • A racing heartbeat.
  • Difficulty passing urine.
  • Agitation, confusion, unsteadiness, hallucinations and delirium; in severe cases seizures or coma.

Symptoms can begin within an hour or so and may last a long time. Anyone showing them after a possible exposure needs urgent medical care.

What about animals?

Belladonna is generally regarded as toxic to dogs, cats, horses and cattle, which may show restlessness, dilated pupils, a fast heart rate, dry mouth and digestive upset. Interestingly, some species are much more tolerant. Many rabbits, for example, carry an enzyme that breaks down atropine, a curiosity that once puzzled naturalists who saw rabbits nibbling the plant unharmed. That tolerance does not extend to the people or pets who might later eat those animals or the plant itself, and it is never a reason to assume a plant is safe.

Who is most at risk?

  • Young children, above all pre-schoolers. The berries are attractive and taste mildly sweet, so a child may eat several before anyone notices. Medical case series from Türkiye and elsewhere describe young children as the most frequent victims.
  • Foragers and berry-pickers who confuse the fruit with bilberries or other wild berries.
  • Home cooks buying or gathering leafy greens such as spinach in areas where belladonna grows wild.
  • People experimenting with "natural highs". The plant's hallucinogenic reputation has led to serious poisonings; there is no safe recreational use.
  • Grazing animals in overgrown pasture or fed contaminated hay.

Safe handling and gardening tips

  1. Learn the star. Teach older children and fellow walkers that a single black berry on a green five-pointed star means "do not touch".
  2. Remove self-sown plants from gardens, allotments and the edges of play areas. Wear gloves, avoid touching your eyes, and wash your hands afterwards.
  3. Dig out the roots rather than simply cutting the stems, and bag the material for household waste rather than leaving it where animals can reach it.
  4. Never forage berries you cannot identify with complete certainty, and supervise children closely in late summer and autumn when fruit is ripe.
  5. Check hay and pasture margins if you keep horses or livestock.

What to do in an emergency

If you suspect someone has eaten deadly nightshade berries or leaves, act immediately, even if there are no symptoms yet.

  • Türkiye: call 112, or the Ulusal Zehir Danışma Merkezi on 114, 24 hours a day.
  • United States: Poison Help on 1-800-222-1222; dial 911 if the person is confused, hallucinating, having a seizure or hard to wake.
  • United Kingdom: 999 in an emergency or NHS 111. Elsewhere in Europe, call 112.
  • Pets and livestock: call your vet or an emergency clinic. In the US, the ASPCA Animal Poison Control Center is on (888) 426-4435 (a fee may apply).

Remove any remaining berries from the mouth, keep the person calm and cool, and take a sample or photo of the plant, ideally showing a berry and its star-shaped calyx. Do not induce vomiting unless a poison centre or doctor tells you to. Hospitals can provide monitoring, supportive care and, in some cases, specific antidote treatment.

Key facts

  • Botanical name: Atropa belladonna (family Solanaceae, the potato and tomato family).
  • Toxic parts: the entire plant, including roots, leaves and berries.
  • Main toxins: tropane alkaloids: atropine, hyoscyamine and scopolamine.
  • Identification clue: single shiny black berries, each sitting on a green five-pointed star.
  • Most at risk: young children attracted by sweet-tasting berries.
  • Classic signs: dilated pupils, dry mouth, flushed hot skin, fast heart rate, confusion.
