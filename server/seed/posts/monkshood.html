Monkshood's hooded blue flowers grace many gardens, yet aconite is called the "queen of poisons". Learn to identify it, handle it safely and spot symptoms.

Late in the summer, when many borders are starting to look tired, monkshood comes into its own. Tall spires of deep blue-violet flowers rise above glossy, deeply cut leaves, each bloom shaped like the cowl of a medieval monk or the visor of a knight's helmet. It is a stunning plant, a favourite of cottage gardeners and bumblebees alike, and perfectly hardy in cold climates. It is also one of the most toxic plants commonly sold in garden centres. Monkshood rewards admiration and punishes carelessness, so here is how to keep firmly on the right side of that line.

Wolfsbane and the hound of hell

Few plants have a mythology as dramatic as Aconitum. In Greek legend, retold by the Roman poet Ovid, aconite sprang from the foaming saliva of Cerberus, the three-headed hound of the underworld, when Heracles dragged him into the daylight. The sorceress Medea was said to have tried to poison the hero Theseus with it. Its other famous common name, wolfsbane, is thought to come from its historical use in baits against wolves, and it later found its way into werewolf fiction. It has long been nicknamed the "queen of poisons", and herbalists have long warned that it is no plant for amateurs.

How to identify monkshood

Common monkshood (Aconitum napellus) and its relatives are upright perennials in the buttercup family, Ranunculaceae.

  • Flowers: borne in tall spikes from midsummer to autumn. Each flower has a large, hood- or helmet-shaped upper sepal arching over the rest. Most are deep blue or violet; there are also white, pink, cream and bicoloured varieties, and yellow-flowered wolfsbanes such as Aconitum lycoctonum.
  • Leaves: dark green, glossy, palmate and deeply divided into narrow, jagged lobes, a little like a delphinium or a buttercup leaf.
  • Height: usually one to one and a half metres.
  • Roots: dark, tuberous roots shaped like small turnips or carrots. The species name napellus means "little turnip".

Out of flower it is easy to confuse with delphiniums, larkspur or hardy geraniums. Delphiniums and larkspur are toxic too, so this is one group of plants where mistaken identity offers little comfort. Historically, aconite roots have also been mistaken for horseradish, a reminder that roots dug from a mixed border should never end up in the kitchen.

Where it grows

Wild monkshoods are native to mountainous and cool regions across Europe and Asia, as well as parts of North America, growing in damp meadows, open woodland and along streams. Germany's Federal Institute for Risk Assessment (BfR) notes that it grows both in gardens and in damp mountain areas. Many species and cultivars are widely sold for gardens, where they thrive in moisture-retentive soil and partial shade.

What makes it dangerous

The whole plant contains aconitine and related alkaloids, and the BfR highlights the root as particularly poisonous. These compounds interfere with sodium channels, the tiny gates that control electrical signals in nerves and heart muscle. They keep those gates open when they should close, which disrupts nerve signals and can cause dangerous heart rhythm disturbances.

What sets monkshood apart from many other toxic garden plants is that the alkaloids can be absorbed through broken skin and mucous membranes. The International Programme on Chemical Safety's monograph on Aconitum notes that after skin or wound contact, tingling may appear first at the point of contact, followed by numbness. Most gardeners who handle the plant briefly suffer no harm, but it is a strong reason to wear gloves.

Worldwide, many of the serious aconite poisonings described in medical literature have involved herbal preparations containing aconite root that were inadequately processed, particularly in parts of Asia where the root is used in traditional medicine. Home remedies made from monkshood are never safe.

The gardener's inquest

In 2014, the death of a 33-year-old estate gardener in Hampshire, England, made headlines after his family suggested he had been poisoned by monkshood growing where he worked. At the inquest in 2015, the coroner recorded an open verdict, saying the evidence was not sufficient to find aconite poisoning. The case proved nothing about monkshood, but it did prompt many gardeners to take a fresh look at how they handle it.

Symptoms in people

Symptoms can begin quickly after swallowing plant material and may include:

  • Burning, tingling and numbness of the mouth, face and throat, which can spread to the limbs.
  • Nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain and diarrhoea.
  • Sweating, dizziness, muscle weakness and visual disturbances.
  • Low blood pressure, palpitations, and a slow, fast or irregular heartbeat.
  • In severe cases, life-threatening arrhythmias and collapse.

Skin contact may cause localised tingling or numbness; seek advice if symptoms spread or you feel unwell.

Symptoms in pets and livestock

Monkshood is toxic to dogs, cats and horses, as well as to cattle and other livestock. Signs include drooling, vomiting, weakness, trembling, difficulty breathing and an irregular heartbeat. Animals usually avoid the bitter living plant, but clippings or uprooted tubers left within reach are a real hazard.

Who is most at risk?

  • Gardeners dividing clumps, deadheading or lifting roots with bare hands.
  • Children who pick the attractive flowers or play with seed heads.
  • Foragers who mistake young leaves or roots for edible plants.
  • People using herbal remedies that may contain aconite root.
  • Pets and livestock with access to garden waste.

Safe gardening tips

  1. Always wear gloves when planting, dividing, cutting back or lifting monkshood, and choose gloves without holes.
  2. Cover cuts and scratches on your hands and forearms before you start.
  3. Wash your hands, tools and gloves afterwards, and avoid touching your face while working.
  4. Keep it away from edibles. Never grow monkshood in or beside a vegetable patch or herb bed, and never store its roots with edible ones.
  5. Label it clearly in shared or community gardens.
  6. Dispose of clippings safely, bagged and out of reach of animals, and do not include it in cut-flower arrangements for homes with young children or pets.
  7. Consider the site. If small children play in the garden, plant monkshood at the back of a border or choose a safer blue flower.

What to do in an emergency

Swallowing any part of monkshood is an emergency. Do not wait for symptoms.

  • Türkiye: call 112, or the Ulusal Zehir Danışma Merkezi on 114, open 24 hours.
  • United States: call 911 for any symptoms, or Poison Help on 1-800-222-1222.
  • United Kingdom: 999 in an emergency or NHS 111 for advice; elsewhere in Europe, 112.
  • Pets and livestock: your vet immediately; in the US, the ASPCA Animal Poison Control Center on (888) 426-4435 (a fee may apply).

For skin contact, the BfR advises rinsing thoroughly with water or mild soapy water; for the eyes, rinse with plenty of water. Remove any plant material from the mouth, take a sample or a photo of the plant to hospital, and do not induce vomiting unless a medical professional tells you to.

Key facts

  • Botanical name: Aconitum napellus and related species (family Ranunculaceae).
  • Common names: monkshood, aconite, wolfsbane, "queen of poisons".
  • Toxic parts: the whole plant, especially the roots.
  • Main toxins: aconitine and related alkaloids acting on nerve and heart sodium channels.
  • Warning signs: tingling and numbness, vomiting, irregular heartbeat.
  • Golden rule: always wear gloves, and keep it far from the vegetable garden.
