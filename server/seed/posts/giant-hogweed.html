Giant hogweed can tower over an adult, and its sap makes skin blister in sunlight. Learn to identify it, avoid its look-alikes and treat exposure.

Most poisonous plants have to be eaten to do harm. Giant hogweed does not need to get anywhere near your mouth. Brush past it in shorts on a sunny day, snap a stem to use as a telescope or a pea-shooter, or strim it without protection, and a day or two later you may be nursing painful blisters that can leave marks for months. Introduced as a garden curiosity, Heracleum mantegazzianum has escaped across Europe and North America and become one of the most recognisable invasive plants in the world. It is a plant you should learn to spot from a distance.

A Victorian curiosity gone rogue

Giant hogweed is native to the western Greater Caucasus, the mountains between the Black Sea and the Caspian. Nineteenth-century plant collectors brought it to Britain and the rest of Europe, where its sheer size made it a sensation in grand gardens. Seeds were swapped and sown, and the plant soon escaped along rivers and railway lines. In 1971 the band Genesis immortalised it in a tongue-in-cheek song, "The Return of the Giant Hogweed", about a monstrous plant taking its revenge on humanity. Real life has been less theatrical but still serious: in 2017 the European Union added giant hogweed to its list of invasive alien species of Union concern, which requires member states to act against its spread, and in Great Britain it is an offence to plant it or cause it to grow in the wild.

How to identify giant hogweed

It is the scale that gives giant hogweed away. Look for:

  • Height: commonly two to five metres when flowering; it can make a tall adult look small.
  • Stems: thick, hollow, ridged and often several centimetres across, heavily blotched with purple-red and covered in coarse, bristly white hairs, particularly at the leaf joints.
  • Leaves: enormous, deeply lobed and jagged, often more than a metre long, sharply pointed at the tips.
  • Flowers: huge, flat-topped or slightly domed umbrellas of small white flowers in early to midsummer, often half a metre or more across.
  • Seeds: large, flat, oval seeds produced in vast numbers. A plant usually flowers once, after a few years of growth, then dies, leaving its seed bank behind.

Look-alikes

  • Common hogweed (Heracleum sphondylium) is much smaller, usually under two metres, with less jagged leaves and stems that are hairy but not heavily purple-blotched. Its sap can also cause milder phototoxic reactions, so do not handle it carelessly either.
  • Cow parsley (Anthriscus sylvestris) is smaller and more delicate, with fern-like leaves, and flowers in spring.
  • Angelica (Angelica sylvestris) has smooth, often purple-flushed stems and rounded flower heads, but lacks the bristles and huge jagged leaves.
  • Sosnowsky's hogweed (Heracleum sosnowskyi), another giant hogweed from the Caucasus, is widespread in Eastern Europe and the Baltic states and is just as hazardous.

Where it grows

Giant hogweed loves moist, disturbed ground: riverbanks and streamsides, where seeds float to new sites, roadsides, railway embankments, waste ground, woodland edges, parks and neglected gardens. It is now established across much of northern and central Europe, the British Isles and parts of Canada and the northern United States.

What makes it dangerous

Giant hogweed sap contains furanocoumarins, chemicals that make skin extremely sensitive to ultraviolet light. When sap on the skin is exposed to sunlight, even on a cloudy day, it causes a reaction called phytophotodermatitis: essentially a chemical burn triggered by light. The same chemistry is behind milder reactions to wild parsnip, rue, and even lime juice spilled on skin in the sun, but giant hogweed produces the reaction on a dramatic scale.

The sap is found throughout the plant, especially in the stems, leaf stalks and flower heads. Simply brushing against intact leaves may not cause a reaction, but breaking stems, cutting, strimming or mowing releases sap that can splash onto skin and clothing.

Symptoms in people

  • Redness and a burning or itching sensation, typically starting within hours of sun exposure, though it can take up to a day or two.
  • Painful blisters, sometimes large, appearing within about 48 hours.
  • Dark or purplish skin discolouration that can last for months or even years, and skin that stays sensitive to sunlight.
  • Possible scarring after severe blistering.
  • Sap in the eyes can cause intense irritation and potentially serious eye injury.

Pets and animals

Dogs and other animals can also be affected, mainly on lightly furred areas such as the nose, mouth, belly and around the eyes, after running through or chewing the plant. If your dog has been in a hogweed patch, wear gloves and wash the exposed areas gently with soap and water, keep the animal out of strong sunlight and call your vet if redness or blisters develop.

Who is most at risk?

  • Children, who are fascinated by the giant stems and may use them as toys, telescopes or swords.
  • Gardeners, landscapers and council workers who cut, strim or mow it without protective clothing.
  • Walkers, anglers, canoeists and campers along riverbanks in summer.
  • Dog owners whose pets crash through riverside vegetation.

Safe handling and control

  1. Look, do not touch. Teach children to recognise the plant and stay clear of it.
  2. Never strim or mow it without full protection. Strimmers fling sap and plant fragments onto skin and clothes.
  3. If you must handle it, wear waterproof gloves, long sleeves and trousers made of water-resistant material, and eye protection or a face shield. Work on overcast days or in the evening if possible.
  4. Remove contaminated clothing carefully and wash it separately; wash tools and gloves while still wearing protection.
  5. Large infestations are best dealt with by professionals. Contact your local council, municipality or invasive species programme for advice; in many places there are rules for its disposal.
  6. Do not let it set seed. Removing flower heads before seeds ripen, safely and with protection, prevents the next generation.

What to do after exposure

  1. Get out of the sun. Cover the affected skin immediately and move indoors or into deep shade.
  2. Wash the area as soon as possible with soap and cool water.
  3. Keep the skin covered from sunlight for at least 48 hours, and follow medical advice about protecting the area in the months that follow.
  4. If sap gets in the eyes, rinse them with plenty of clean water, wear sunglasses, and seek medical help promptly.
  5. Seek medical advice for blistering, burns over a large area, or any eye exposure.

Where to get help:

  • Türkiye: 112 in an emergency, or the Ulusal Zehir Danışma Merkezi on 114 for advice, 24 hours a day.
  • United States: Poison Help, 1-800-222-1222.
  • United Kingdom: NHS 111 (NHS 24 on 111 in Scotland), or 999 in an emergency; elsewhere in Europe, 112.
  • Pets: your vet; in the US, the ASPCA Animal Poison Control Center on (888) 426-4435 (a fee may apply).

Do not burst blisters, and if a child or pet may have chewed the plant, mention this when you call, as it can irritate the mouth and lips too.

Key facts

  • Botanical name: Heracleum mantegazzianum (family Apiaceae).
  • Native range: the western Greater Caucasus; invasive across Europe and North America.
  • Hazard: sap containing furanocoumarins causes burns and blisters when skin is exposed to sunlight.
  • ID clues: up to five metres tall, purple-blotched bristly stems, huge jagged leaves, giant white umbrellas of flowers.
  • First aid: cover, wash with soap and cool water, keep out of the sun for at least 48 hours.
  • Never strim or cut it without full protective clothing.
