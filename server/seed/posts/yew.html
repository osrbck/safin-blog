The English yew can live for millennia, yet its needles and seeds are deadly to people, horses and pets. Here is how to enjoy this churchyard tree safely.

Walk into an old English churchyard and there is a good chance a yew will be waiting for you: dark, broad and impossibly ancient, its trunk twisted and hollowed, its branches heavy with flat green needles and, in autumn, dotted with little red cups. Yews are some of the oldest living things in Europe, trees that may have been mature when the stones around them were laid. They are also, almost entirely, poisonous. Taxus baccata is a tree that invites reverence and demands respect in equal measure.

A tree of time, war and medicine

The Fortingall Yew in Perthshire, Scotland, is often described as one of the oldest trees in Europe. Estimates of its age vary enormously, from about two thousand years to considerably more, because hollow yews leave no complete set of rings to count. Why yews and churchyards go together is still debated: theories range from the tree as a symbol of everlasting life to the practical idea that a poisonous tree inside a walled churchyard kept wandering cattle away. Some churchyard yews may even predate the churches beside them.

Yew wood is famously strong and springy. The Neolithic man known as Ötzi, whose body was found in the Alps, carried an unfinished yew longbow more than five thousand years ago, and the English longbow of the Middle Ages was traditionally made from yew. In the twentieth century the genus found an unexpected new purpose: the cancer drug paclitaxel was first isolated from the bark of the Pacific yew (Taxus brevifolia). Today it is produced largely from compounds extracted from the renewable needles of cultivated yews, including European yew, and from plant cell cultures, in pharmaceutical facilities. None of this makes any part of a garden yew safe to eat.

How to identify English yew

  • Form: an evergreen conifer that can grow into a large, spreading tree, but is more often seen clipped into hedges, topiary or dense shrubs.
  • Needles: flat, soft, one to four centimetres long, dark glossy green above and paler, yellow-green beneath, arranged spirally but twisted so they appear to lie in two flat rows along the twig.
  • Bark: thin, reddish-brown and flaking, often on a fluted, multi-stemmed trunk.
  • "Berries": yews are usually either male or female. Female trees bear a single seed partly enclosed in a fleshy, bright red, cup-shaped structure called an aril, open at one end so the dark seed is visible inside.

Yews are planted in countless variations, including upright Irish yews, golden-leaved forms and the hybrid Taxus × media, along with Japanese yew (Taxus cuspidata), which is common in North American landscaping. All of them should be treated as poisonous.

Where it grows

English yew is native to much of Europe, north-west Africa and south-west Asia, including the forests of northern Türkiye, where it is known as porsuk. It grows wild in woodland, often on chalk and limestone, and is planted almost everywhere as a hedge, in parks, cemeteries, school grounds and gardens. Because it tolerates heavy clipping, it is one of the most popular hedging plants in temperate gardens.

What makes it dangerous

Yews contain taxine alkaloids, especially taxine A and taxine B, which act mainly on the heart. According to Ontario's agriculture ministry, they disrupt the movement of sodium and calcium in heart muscle cells, leading to dangerous rhythm disturbances. The needles and the seeds are highly poisonous. The one notable exception is the red, fleshy aril itself, which is not considered toxic, but it surrounds a poisonous seed, so the "berry" as a whole must never be eaten.

Three features make yew especially treacherous:

  • It is toxic all year, fresh or dried. Dead needles and old clippings remain dangerous.
  • It can act very quickly. In animals, sudden death within a few hours of eating yew is the most commonly reported outcome, sometimes with no warning signs.
  • There is no specific antidote, so prevention matters more than treatment.

Symptoms in people

  • Nausea, vomiting and abdominal pain.
  • Dizziness, dry mouth, dilated pupils and weakness.
  • A slow or irregular heartbeat, low blood pressure and difficulty breathing.
  • Collapse, seizures or loss of consciousness in serious cases.

Children most often come to medical attention after eating the bright red arils. A seed swallowed whole may pass without releasing its toxin, but chewed seeds are a different story, so every ingestion deserves an immediate call for advice.

Symptoms in animals

Yew is toxic to dogs, cats, horses, cattle, sheep, goats and many other animals. Horses are particularly vulnerable. Ontario's guidance describes sudden death, often within two to three hours of ingestion, as the most common finding, with animals frequently found dead next to yew bushes or discarded clippings. Before death, animals may show trembling, incoordination, nervousness, difficulty breathing, a slow heart rate, vomiting, diarrhoea or convulsions. Interestingly, deer, moose and elk can browse yew without harm, which sometimes misleads people into thinking it is safe for livestock.

Who is most at risk?

  • Horses, cattle, sheep and goats with access to hedges, churchyards, parks or clippings.
  • Dogs that chew sticks or play with yew twigs and fallen arils.
  • Young children attracted to the red "berries".
  • Gardeners and hedge trimmers who leave clippings where animals can reach them.
  • Households at Christmas that use yew branches in wreaths and garlands.

Safe gardening and land-management tips

  1. Never plant yew next to paddocks, pastures, or where branches could overhang a field or track.
  2. Collect every clipping after hedge cutting. Sweep up, bag and dispose of trimmings with household or municipal green waste. Never throw them over a fence, onto a manure heap or into a field.
  3. Do not hang yew wreaths on stable doors, gates or fence posts, or anywhere animals can reach.
  4. Wear gloves when pruning and wash your hands afterwards; yew sap and dust can irritate skin.
  5. Teach children that red yew "berries" are not for eating, and sweep up fallen arils from patios and play areas in autumn.
  6. Riding out? Do not let horses snatch at hedges along lanes or churchyard walls.

What to do in an emergency

If you suspect that a person or animal has eaten yew needles, seeds or clippings, act at once.

  • Türkiye: call 112, or the Ulusal Zehir Danışma Merkezi on 114, 24 hours a day.
  • United States: Poison Help, 1-800-222-1222; call 911 for fainting, collapse or breathing difficulty.
  • United Kingdom: 999 in an emergency or NHS 111; elsewhere in Europe, 112.
  • Animals: call your vet immediately, before any signs appear. In the US, the ASPCA Animal Poison Control Center is on (888) 426-4435 (a fee may apply).

Move animals away from the source, take a twig or a photo with you, and do not induce vomiting unless a doctor or vet tells you to.

Key facts

  • Botanical name: Taxus baccata (family Taxaceae); Turkish porsuk.
  • Toxic parts: needles, bark, wood and seeds, fresh or dried; only the red aril flesh is considered non-toxic.
  • Main toxins: taxine alkaloids, which affect the heart.
  • Main danger: sudden heart failure, especially in horses and livestock.
  • Biggest preventable hazard: hedge clippings dumped where animals can reach them.
  • Surprising fact: the cancer drug paclitaxel was discovered in yew bark.
