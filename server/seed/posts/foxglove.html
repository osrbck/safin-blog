Foxglove gave medicine one of its oldest heart drugs, and its leaves are seriously toxic. Here is how to grow this cottage-garden favourite safely.

Few flowers look as much like a storybook illustration as foxglove. Tall spires of pink-purple bells, each one freckled inside with dark spots, rise above woodland clearings and cottage borders in early summer, humming with bumblebees that crawl right inside the blooms. Old country names such as fairy gloves, folk's gloves and dead men's bells capture its double nature: enchanting, and a little sinister. Digitalis purpurea is a plant that has saved countless lives in the hands of physicians and endangered others in the hands of the unwary.

From folk remedy to heart medicine

The botanical name Digitalis, from the Latin for "finger", was coined by the German botanist Leonhart Fuchs in 1542, a nod to how neatly the flowers fit over a fingertip. Its medical fame came later. In 1785 the English physician William Withering published An Account of the Foxglove, drawing on 156 of his own cases to show that carefully prepared leaf could relieve "dropsy", the fluid build-up we now recognise as a sign of heart failure. Accounts of how he first learned of the remedy vary; one popular version credits a folk herbalist in Shropshire.

Withering's most important lesson was caution. He found that small, carefully adjusted amounts worked best and that the margin between help and harm was narrow. That observation still holds. The compounds descended from his work, digitoxin from purple foxglove and digoxin from woolly foxglove (Digitalis lanata), remain in medical use today, prescribed in precise doses and monitored with blood tests. The wild plant offers no such precision, which is exactly why it is dangerous.

How to identify foxglove

Foxglove is usually a biennial. That life cycle matters, because the plant looks completely different in each of its two years.

  • Year one: a low rosette of soft, oval to lance-shaped leaves, grey-green, with a velvety, slightly wrinkled surface and a downy underside. There are no flowers.
  • Year two: a single tall flower spike, often one to two metres high, carrying dozens of tubular bells that all hang from one side of the stem. Flowers are usually pink-purple, sometimes white, with spotted throats.
  • After flowering: small, rounded seed capsules packed with tiny seeds. Plants self-seed generously.

The dangerous moment is year one, when there are no flowers to give the game away. Foxglove rosettes have been confused with the leaves of comfrey (Symphytum officinale) and borage (Borago officinalis), both of which some people gather for teas or cooking. Published case reports describe exactly this mix-up. In England, a woman developed a serious heart rhythm disturbance after brewing tea from leaves she believed were comfrey; in two separate Italian cases, people became ill after eating a savoury pie and home-made stuffed pasta filled with leaves they had taken for borage, in one case gathered in winter when neither plant was in flower. A useful clue: borage and comfrey leaves feel bristly or rough, while foxglove leaves feel soft and velvety. But touch is not a reliable test for a beginner, and the safest rule is simple: if you are not certain, do not eat it.

Where it grows

Purple foxglove is native to western and south-western Europe and thrives in acidic soils, woodland edges, heaths, hedgebanks and freshly cleared ground. It has escaped from gardens across much of the temperate world, including North America and New Zealand. Several related wild species, such as the rusty foxglove (Digitalis ferruginea), grow from southern Europe into the Balkans and Anatolia, and many ornamental hybrids are sold in garden centres everywhere.

What makes it dangerous

All parts of foxglove contain cardiac glycosides, including digitoxin, and the leaves are a particularly concentrated source. These compounds slow the heart and make each beat more forceful, which is useful in carefully measured medical treatment but dangerous when the amount is unknown. In poisoning they can trigger a very slow heart rate, irregular rhythms and high blood potassium. Because the toxins are stable, dried leaves remain poisonous.

Symptoms in people

Symptoms usually appear within a few hours of eating plant material and may include:

  • Nausea, vomiting, loss of appetite, abdominal pain and diarrhoea.
  • Headache, dizziness, weakness, confusion or unusual drowsiness.
  • Visual changes, such as blurred vision, halos around lights, or a yellow-green tint to the world. These are classic signs of digitalis toxicity.
  • A slow, irregular or pounding heartbeat, fainting or collapse.

Touching foxglove is not considered dangerous for most people, although some gardeners report skin irritation and it is sensible to wash your hands after handling it.

Symptoms in pets

Foxglove is toxic to dogs, cats and horses. Animals may drool, vomit, develop diarrhoea, seem weak or lethargic, or show an abnormal heart rate. Fortunately, the leaves are bitter, so most animals leave them alone, but young dogs that chew anything, and grazing animals offered clippings or contaminated hay, are at real risk.

Who is most at risk?

  • Foragers and herbal-tea makers collecting comfrey, borage or other "wild greens" before plants flower.
  • Young children, who may suck the nectar-bearing flowers or chew on seed heads.
  • Puppies and curious pets in gardens where foxglove self-seeds freely.
  • Horses and livestock grazing rough pasture or fed hay cut from weedy fields.

Growing foxglove safely

Foxgloves are superb plants for pollinators and woodland gardens, and many families grow them without incident. A few habits keep it that way:

  1. Never grow foxglove next to comfrey or borage, or in a herb or vegetable bed where leaves might be harvested by mistake.
  2. Label plants in shared or community gardens, particularly while they are still leafy rosettes.
  3. Wear gloves if you have sensitive skin, and wash your hands after weeding or deadheading.
  4. Cut spent flower spikes before seed sets if you want to limit self-seeding near paths or play areas.
  5. Keep clippings out of reach of animals and away from hay meadows.
  6. Talk to children about why flowers are not snacks, however pretty they are.

What to do in an emergency

If anyone may have eaten foxglove, including in a tea, salad or cooked dish, get advice straight away, even if they feel fine.

  • Türkiye: call 112, or the Ulusal Zehir Danışma Merkezi (National Poison Information Centre) on 114, available around the clock.
  • United States: Poison Help, 1-800-222-1222; call 911 for collapse, seizures or breathing difficulty.
  • United Kingdom: 999 in an emergency, or NHS 111. Across Europe, 112 reaches emergency services.
  • Pets: contact your vet or nearest emergency clinic. In the US, the ASPCA Animal Poison Control Center is on (888) 426-4435 (a fee may apply).

Bring the plant, or a clear photo of its leaves and any flowers, plus any leftover food or tea. Do not induce vomiting unless a medical professional tells you to. Hospitals can monitor the heart and have specific antidote treatments for digitalis-type poisoning.

Key facts

  • Botanical name: Digitalis purpurea (family Plantaginaceae).
  • Toxic parts: the whole plant, especially the leaves; fresh or dried.
  • Main toxins: cardiac glycosides, including digitoxin.
  • Classic warning signs: vomiting, slow or irregular heartbeat, yellow-green or blurred vision.
  • Look-alikes: comfrey and borage leaves before flowering.
  • Historical claim to fame: William Withering's 1785 account launched digitalis as a heart medicine.
